What is Land Pollution?

Land pollution occurs when waste, chemicals, or other harmful materials contaminate or degrade the land and soil. It may result from household garbage, industrial and hazardous waste, agricultural chemicals, petroleum products, construction debris, mining waste, and improperly managed wastewater.

Healthy soil supports plant growth, stores and filters water, cycles nutrients, and provides habitats for many organisms. When pollutants enter the soil, these natural functions may be damaged. Contaminants can also remain in the environment for long periods and may eventually reach crops, groundwater, rivers, animals, and people. (United Nations Environment Programme, 2021)

Causes of Land Pollution

In the Philippines, one major cause of land pollution is the improper disposal of solid waste. This includes littering, illegal dumping, leaving garbage in open dumps, burning waste, and disposing of unsegregated waste in places that are not authorised to receive it.

Republic Act No. 9003, or the Ecological Solid Waste Management Act of 2000, requires waste segregation, recycling, composting, proper collection, and environmentally safe disposal. The law prohibits littering, open burning, open dumping, collecting unsegregated waste, and dumping waste outside authorised facilities. Despite these requirements, improper waste disposal remains a problem in many communities. (Republic Act No. 9003, 2001)

Other causes include

  • Poor management of household and commercial waste
  • Improper disposal of industrial chemicals and hazardous materials
  • Excessive or incorrect use of pesticides and fertilisers
  • Disposal of electronic waste, batteries, oil, and medical waste with ordinary garbage
  • Mining activities and the release of mine tailings
  • Construction debris and uncontrolled urban development
  • Limited waste-collection, recycling, composting, and disposal facilities
  • Increasing population, consumption, and use of disposable products

The United Nations Environment Programme identifies industrial activities, municipal and livestock waste, agrochemicals, and petroleum products as major human sources of soil pollution. (UNEP, 2020)

Effects of Land Pollution

Land pollution affects the environment and human communities in several ways.

Soil degradation and reduced agricultural productivity

Pollutants can disrupt the organisms and nutrients that keep soil healthy. High concentrations of toxic substances may reduce crop yields or make the land unsuitable for agriculture.

Contamination of food and water

Chemicals and heavy metals in polluted soil may be absorbed by crops. Rainwater can also carry pollutants into rivers, lakes, and groundwater. This creates possible risks for people who consume contaminated food or water. (UNEP, Land and Soil Pollution)

Damage to ecosystems

Polluted land may destroy habitats, harm plants and animals, reduce biodiversity, and weaken the ability of ecosystems to recover from floods, droughts, and other environmental pressures.

Health risks

People may be exposed to pollutants through contaminated food, drinking water, dust, smoke from burning waste, or direct contact with hazardous materials. Waste workers, nearby communities, and children can be particularly vulnerable. The World Health Organization states that improper waste disposal can contaminate soil, water, and air and contribute to harmful health outcomes. (World Health Organization, 2024)

Flooding and disease

Garbage discarded in canals and drainage systems can block the flow of water and worsen flooding. Accumulated waste and standing water may also provide breeding places for disease-carrying mosquitoes and other pests.

Current Status

The Environmental Management Bureau estimated that the Philippines would generate approximately 60,756.55 tons of solid waste per day in 2025 — around 22.18 million tons in one year. This number is a projection based partly on regional population data, not a complete measurement of all waste collected nationwide. (Convention on Biological Diversity Online Reporting Tool, Philippine government submission)

There has been progress in local waste planning. As of 2025, the DENR-EMB reported that 1,459 of 1,592 cities, municipalities, and provinces — approximately 92% — had approved ten-year solid-waste management plans. Nevertheless, having an approved plan does not automatically mean that waste is properly segregated, collected, recycled, and disposed of in every community.

Hazardous waste is another concern. According to the Philippine Statistics Authority, the country generated 269.55 thousand tons of hazardous waste in 2024. Oil accounted for the largest share, followed by miscellaneous waste and waste containing inorganic chemicals. (Philippine Statistics Authority, 2025)

These figures show that land pollution remains a serious environmental challenge. Addressing it requires proper segregation at source, reliable waste collection, functioning materials-recovery facilities, safe treatment of hazardous waste, enforcement against illegal dumping, and greater participation from households, businesses, industries, and local governments.
